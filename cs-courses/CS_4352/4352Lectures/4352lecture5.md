## **Qualitative Research Methods for Need Finding**

---

### Introduction to Qualitative Research

Qualitative research uses triangulation to collect data through diverse methods. While qualitative methods are subjective (observations, interviews, focus groups), quantitative methods are more objective and based on numerical data. The purpose of need finding is primarily served through qualitative approaches.

### Observation Techniques

- Observation involves watching users in their natural environment while trying to remain unnoticed
- "Fly on the wall" technique involves no interaction with users
- Goal is to learn normal reactions without influencing the social context
- Particularly helpful in situations where being observed might change user behavior

### Note-Taking Guidelines

- Define what you're trying to learn about
- Identify who you're observing (pseudonyms can be used)
- Capture main issues or themes that stand out
- Use recording technology rather than relying on memory
- Teams can be used for recording at UTD

### Effective Interviewing Process

- Start with introductions, explain roles and purpose
- Get consent to record the interview
- Make participants feel comfortable to encourage open responses
- Listen actively when users answer questions
- Ask probing "why" and "how" questions to get interesting answers
- Ask follow-up questions for clarification
- Keep the focus on the user, not the interviewer

### Building Rapport

- Body language is crucial during interviews
- Open posture is best (avoid casual poses or having obstacles between you and participant)
- End interviews by asking for suggestions or final thoughts
- Close respectfully and exchange contact details for potential follow-up

### Action Items

- [ ] Use recording tools rather than relying on memory when conducting interviews
- [ ] Obtain consent before recording any user sessions
- [ ] Practice appropriate body language to build rapport during interviews

## **Need Finding: User Observation and Interview Techniques**

---

### Introduction to Need Finding

- Need finding is the process of discovering user needs through observation and interviews
- Goal is to create designs and products that genuinely meet user needs
- Cannot rely on assumptions - must observe and interview users directly

### Importance of Observation

- Observation is more valuable than simply asking users what they want
- Example: Walmart learned users said they wanted less cluttered aisles, but actually purchased items from that "clutter"
- Wide gap exists between what users say they do and what they actually do
- Observation provides more accurate insights than self-reporting

### Effective Interview Techniques

- Avoid binary yes/no questions in favor of open-ended questions
- Make questions concrete rather than hypothetical (e.g., "How much did you exercise this week?" vs. "How often do you exercise?")
- Use silence strategically - pausing after questions encourages users to elaborate
- Remember users are experts in their own experiences, not in design solutions
- Avoid asking users to design solutions themselves

### Selecting Interview Participants

- Target representatives from the intended audience of the system
- Include diverse users within the target group
- If ideal participants aren't available, approximate with similar users (e.g., medical students instead of doctors)
- For projects in this course, use personal social networks to find participants without spending money
- Professional research often compensates participants, especially for specialized knowledge

### Advanced Concepts

- Consider "extreme users" to test design limits (e.g., users who get thousands of emails daily)
- Create personas to make target users seem real and develop empathy
- Personas help designers maintain focus and create coherent designs

### Special Approaches

- Some advanced users ("lead users") can contribute to design ideas
- Participatory design/co-design involves users as equals to designers
- These approaches are exceptions to the typical user-designer relationship

## **Effective vs. Ineffective Interviewing Techniques Demonstration**

---

### Key Interviewing Principles

- Based on Stanley Carlyle's 1996 book "Interviews," a good interviewer should be:
  - Knowledgeable about the topic
  - Structured (clear introduction and conclusion)
  - Clear (simple, short questions without jargon)
  - Gentle (allows completion of answers, tolerates pauses)
  - Sensitive (attentive listening, empathetic)
  - Open (responsive to interviewee's priorities)
  - Flexible yet steering (guiding while adapting)
  - Critical (challenging inconsistencies)
  - Remembers previous responses
  - Interprets and clarifies meaning
  - Balanced in talking time
  - Ethically sensitive (explains purpose, ensures confidentiality)

### Poor Interview Example Issues

- Minimal introduction with no context or ethical considerations
- Poor rapport building with limited eye contact
- Reading questions mechanically without engagement
- Accepting short answers without follow-up questions
- Abrupt topic changes without probing for depth
- Yes/no questions that limit detailed responses
- Sudden ending without allowing additional input
- Interviewee appeared uncomfortable and provided minimal information

### Effective Interview Demonstration

- Comprehensive introduction explaining research purpose
- Clear ethical framework (consent, right to stop, confidentiality)
- Strong rapport building creating comfortable atmosphere
- Open-ended questions encouraging detailed responses
- Appropriate follow-up questions and probes
- Requests for specific examples and stories
- Active listening and responding to interviewee comments
- Proper conclusion with opportunity for questions
- Resulted in richer, more detailed responses from interviewee

### Key Takeaways

- Effective interviewing requires preparation, structure and interpersonal skills
- Building rapport significantly improves the quality of information obtained
- Follow-up questions and probes are essential for depth
- Ethical considerations should be explicitly addressed
- The interviewer's approach directly impacts the interviewee's comfort and openness

## **Need-Finding Project Instructions**

---

### Project Overview

This need-finding project requires teams to observe users performing activities in their environments and conduct interviews to identify user needs. The focus should be on understanding user needs and goals rather than designing solutions.

### Required Steps

- Team selects an activity to observe
- Each team member selects at least one individual to observe in their environment
- Remote observations via Teams or Zoom are acceptable
- Conduct 10-15 minute interviews with users
- Take detailed notes during observations and interviews
- Team brainstorms specific user needs based on collected data

### Alternative Approach

If observations are not possible, team members can conduct additional interviews instead.

### Deliverables

- Documentation of each team member's observations and interviews
- Notes from each team member's research
- List of at least three user needs identified through research

### Important Guidelines

- Team lead should coordinate the final submission
- Non-contributing team members will receive a zero grade
- The project can still be successful even if some members don't contribute

### Action Items

- [ ] Select an activity to observe as a team
- [ ] Each team member select at least one individual to observe
- [ ] Conduct observations in users' environments (in-person or remote)
- [ ] Perform 10-15 minute interviews and take notes
- [ ] Brainstorm list of user needs as a team
- [ ] Document all observations, interviews and notes
- [ ] Identify at least three key user needs

## **Understanding Users in Design Process**

---

This presentation explores the fundamental principles of user-centered design (UCD) and methods for understanding user needs to create effective products.

### User Categories and Classifications

- Primary users: directly interact with the product
- Secondary users: influenced by or affect product development
- Users can be classified based on:
  - Physical characteristics (age, sex, culture)
  - Technical proficiency (computer/IT experience)
  - Psychological factors (motivations, attitudes)
- Skill levels range from novice users to expert frequent users
- Physical limitations must be considered (15-35% of population in developed countries have impairments)

### Usability Guidelines

- Products should be intuitive, obvious, efficient, and learnable
- Systems should be memorable, error-resistant, and transparent
- Example of poor usability: Windows estimation feature with unpredictable time calculations
- Challenges include: diverse platforms, varying user types, lack of standards, and performance issues

### Understanding User Needs

- Three types of user needs:
  - Express needs: what users say they want
  - Felt needs: users are unsure of system capabilities
  - Normative needs: professional view of what's needed
- Challenge: users often don't know what's possible or can't clearly communicate their goals
- Notable quotes referenced from Henry Ford and Steve Jobs suggesting direct user input has limitations

### Effective User Research Methods

- Observe existing tasks and user behaviors
- Study users in context to understand required information
- Examine collaborations and motivations
- Propose efficient tasks rooted in existing behaviors
- Create personas with specific details to communicate with stakeholders
- Develop realistic metrics and prototype based on use case scenarios

### Design Thinking Process

- Designers must bridge the gap between user and designer mental models
- Design thinking helps reduce this gap (noted as topic for next meeting)

## **Design Thinking Process Overview**

---

This module explains the design thinking process developed by Stanford's Institute of Design, which consists of five key stages.

### Empathize

- Understand users and care about their lives to create meaningful innovation
- Observe users in the context of their lives and interactions
- Engage with users to elicit stories and insights
- Ask questions about how they complete tasks and what they're thinking

### Define

- Craft a meaningful and actionable problem statement based on user insights
- Analyze and synthesize observations from the empathize stage
- A good problem statement should:
  - Provide focus and frame the problem clearly
  - Inspire the team
  - Inform criteria for evaluating competing ideas
  - Empower independent decision-making
  - Be discrete rather than too broad

### Ideate

- Generate a broad range of possible solutions to the problem statement
- Focus on quantity of ideas rather than finding the "right" idea
- Brainstorm with boundaries while maintaining an open mind
- Consider all possibilities before evaluating feasibility

### Prototype

- Build to think and test to learn
- Create iterative artifacts intended to answer questions
- Experimental phase aimed at identifying the best possible solution
- Learn through user interactions with the prototype

### Test

- Get feedback from users about the prototypes
- Further opportunity to gain empathy for users
- Rigorously test to identify gaps between user needs and the final product
- Use feedback to redefine designs as needed
  While the process appears linear, in practice it's more flexible and iterative. Different teams may work on multiple stages concurrently, and feedback from testing often leads to refinements in earlier stages.

## **Designing Technology for Humans as They Are**

---

The speaker presents a philosophical argument about how we should approach technology design in relation to human cognition. Their perspective challenges popular views on cognitive biases and argues for more collaborative human-centered design.

### Key Design Philosophy

The speaker emphasizes that we must design systems for people as they are, not as we wish them to be. Technology should function as a collaborator with humans rather than viewing people as deficient and needing replacement by machines.

### Critique of "Cognitive Biases"

The speaker disagrees with Daniel Kahneman's framing of human cognitive patterns as "biases" in his book "Thinking Fast and Thinking Slow." Instead of seeing these patterns as errors to be corrected, the speaker argues they are evolutionarily beneficial features of human cognition that work well in real-world contexts.
For example, the "recency bias" (giving more weight to recent events) makes sense because recent events are often most likely to impact current circumstances.

### Alternative Approach to Technology Design

Rather than designing technology that tries to "correct" human biases, the speaker advocates for technology that:

- Takes advantage of natural human cognitive processes
- Supports human judgment rather than replacing it
- Offers suggestions when human judgment might benefit from additional perspectives
- Works collaboratively with humans as a supportive partner
  The speaker believes most human judgment is extremely powerful and correct in contextual real-world situations, unlike the artificial problems used in psychology experiments.

## **Design Thinking: A Process for Better Problem Solving**

---

This presentation introduces design thinking as a powerful approach to solving problems of all sizes. Developed at Stanford, design thinking provides a structured methodology that broadens perspective and leads to innovative solutions.

### What is Design Thinking?

Design thinking is a user-centered approach to problem-solving that encourages exploration of alternatives and creation of entirely new options. It focuses on understanding the context and culture of all stakeholders involved, going beyond mere ergonomics.

### The Design Thinking Process

- Research through direct observation and qualitative data collection to generate empathy-building stories
- Define the real problem based on research insights
- Ideate without constraints of existing solutions
- Create simple prototypes to test ideas
- Repeat the ideate-prototype-test cycle early to make mistakes quickly and inexpensively

### Benefits of Design Thinking

- Provides a method and common language for multidisciplinary collaboration
- Leads to greater creativity and innovation
- Produces better solutions faster
- Makes complex problems more manageable through a structured approach
- Accelerates innovation through rapid prototyping and real-world testing

## **ATM Customer Segmentation and Persona Creation Instructions**

---

This meeting provided instructions for a user segmentation exercise focused on ATM customers.

### Task Overview

The exercise involves analyzing a table with two columns: user characteristics and ATM customer characteristics. Participants must segment the ATM customers into two or more distinct groups based on the user characteristics.

### Segmentation Process

- Take the user characteristics (left column) and use them to split ATM customers (right column) into multiple groups
- Each resulting group should include all relevant characteristics from the original ATM customer profile
- The characteristics should be distributed in a way that makes logical sense for each segment

### Example Provided

The instructor demonstrated segmentation based on age:

- Group 1: Ages 12-65
- Group 2: Ages 65-80+
- All other relevant ATM characteristics would then be distributed between these two groups

### Persona Development

- After completing the segmentation, participants should select one segment
- Create a detailed persona representing a typical user from that segment
- ATM customer groups should not be named until this persona creation phase
